# The laws of video production

These eleven laws apply to every look and length, from a short clip to a feature film. A long work adds a work level above the scenes so they can share the same decisions and references.

## Law 1. The brief's look governs every frame

Use the medium and look the brief names, including any required method of capture, and take its words at their ordinary meaning: a film or cinematic look is live action as a camera would record it, a cartoon is animation in the named style. Each order starts from its own brief. The look of an earlier video, a planning drawing or a habit of the team never decides this one. Turn ambiguous style words into observable choices with agreed examples or the responsible person's intent, and keep those choices in every frame.

## Law 2. Planning material explains, it does not dictate

A planning sketch or storyboard exists so everyone understands the scenes: what happens, in what order, where the camera is and what matters. It may be drawn in any style and is never the material the shots are made from, and it is never copied frame by frame: the shots are made fresh in the brief's look. An approved production asset, such as a character sheet, a set plan or a product photo, is used directly when its look, rights and purpose fit the brief. Where the made video tells the story better than the plan, the video wins.

## Law 3. What matters happens on screen

Every key event of the story, such as an arrival, a meeting, a change or a reveal, happens inside a shot, in view and with its motion. It is never left to a cut between two shots to imply. Pace comes from the action and the camera inside the shots, not from cutting shots short. The edit and the sound support what the shot shows, and replace it only where the brief asks for that effect.

## Law 4. One world, held still

Record what viewers must recognize from shot to shot before making anything: identities, relative sizes, wardrobe, movement, light and the physics of the chosen look. Keep them consistent unless the brief calls for a change. Make shots in a way that preserves them, such as fewer and longer continuous shots, or a shot that continues from the accepted last frame of the one before.

## Law 5. The set is built before it is filmed

For any work that shows a space, real or drawn, build a shared set plan before making or editing shots where the layout matters: a measured 3D layout of walls, openings, floor and main objects as plain shapes, with named markers for where each character stands or moves, where the camera is and goes, and where each key event happens. Characters stand in it as plain figures at their real height, so sizes come from the set and not from a sentence. Without a 3D tool, a measured floor plan with the same markers serves; a screen recording maps its screen, with the places of actions and overlays. The set fixes where things are, never how the video looks (Law 2). Use it to plan shots, align the cut and check continuity.

## Law 6. A keeper holds the look and the world

Each exact clip in the cut needs the keeper's recorded acceptance against the agreed look, continuity records and relevant set markers, including motion when it matters, and the keeper checks that no shot is a still picture passed off as motion. A deliberate break must serve the director's intent and have a recorded reason. Material drift goes back to its owner and is made again, then checked again. On a long work, a lead keeper owns the common references that scene keepers use.

## Law 7. Each tool does what it does best

Use an authorized tool and access route that reliably produces the motion, light, matter and performance the work requires. Exact geometry, such as a picture inside a screen, an interface, logos, titles or an end card, is made in the edit from approved sources, not left to generation. Real actions are captured when the audience must see the real thing. Sound belongs with the action from the start, including intentional silence.

## Law 8. Prove the hardest moment first

Before full production, make a short probe of the hardest moment in the chosen look and method, often a few seconds, in enough context to judge the risk. When the choice of tool is uncertain, compare two or three methods on the same references and conditions within the agreed budget. The head and keeper accept the result as the shared baseline for the look and method; a materially different risk calls for its own probe. Keep the probe, method, settings and cost so the next choice has evidence.

## Law 9. The platform shapes the frame

Compose for the destination's verified format, viewing area and playback conditions. Keep essential action, words and sound accessible there. For example, a short vertical video uses the vertical format, hooks in its first second, keeps important details clear of the platform's buttons and stays readable without sound.

## Law 10. The whole work is finished as one

Finish color, contrast, grain, sound and titles together across the assembled work, each by one owner and against one look reference. Use the probe (Law 8) to establish that reference. Keep reference frames for each scene where useful and a color script for a long work when its look changes on purpose. Compare adjacent shots by sight and sound and measure properties that matter, such as levels, color balance, skin tones, loudness, voices and room tone. An underlying failure of world, scale or motion (Laws 4 to 6) must be repaired at its source, with the shot made again where necessary.

## Law 11. Judge the finished video, not the effort

The head judges the exact version intended for delivery, as the audience will see and hear it: first whole at normal speed, then at every cut and key event at the detail needed, against the brief and one question per law. A video that breaks a law goes back to the owner who can fix it, however much it cost. That acceptance belongs to the finished version and is established again after a repair.
