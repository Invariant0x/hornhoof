# The laws of video production

These eleven laws apply to every look and length, from a short clip to a feature film. A long work adds a work level above the scenes so they can share the same decisions and references.

## Law 1. The brief's look governs every frame

The medium and look must serve the brief, including any required method of capture. Agree on what the audience should see and hear and hold to those choices throughout the work.

## Law 2. Planning material explains, it does not dictate

A planning sketch or storyboard communicates action, order, camera and emphasis in whatever form makes them clear. Use an approved production asset directly when its look, rights and purpose fit the brief. Adapt the plan when it helps the finished video tell the intended story.

## Law 3. What matters happens on screen

Make every required event understandable to the audience. Choose whether the shot, the edit or the sound conveys it. Show the motion itself when seeing how it happens is part of the brief.

## Law 4. One world, held still

The identities, relative sizes, movement, light and physics of the chosen look must stay recognizable from shot to shot. An intentional change must serve the work as agreed in the brief.

## Law 5. The set is built before it is filmed

A shared set plan must guide shot choices where the layout matters. Fixed features, paths, viewpoints and events must line up across shots so the audience can follow the space.

## Law 6. A keeper holds the look and the world

Each exact clip in the cut needs the keeper's recorded acceptance against the agreed look, continuity records and relevant set markers, including motion when it matters. A deliberate break must serve the director's intent and have a recorded reason. Material drift needs correction and a new check. On a long work, a lead keeper owns the common references that scene keepers use.

## Law 7. Each tool does what it does best

An authorized tool and access route must reliably produce and preserve the motion, performance, sound and precise visual details the work requires. The result must preserve real actions and approved source details when they matter. Sound belongs with the action from the start, including intentional silence.

## Law 8. Prove the hardest moment first

The hardest moment must be proven in enough context to judge the risk before full production. Comparisons need equivalent evidence and must stay within the agreed budget. The head and keeper accept the result as the shared baseline for the look and method. A materially different risk calls for its own evidence. Keep the result, method and cost so the next choice has evidence.

## Law 9. The platform shapes the frame

Compose for the destination's verified format, viewing area and playback conditions. Keep essential action, words and sound accessible there.

## Law 10. The whole work is finished as one

Finish color, contrast, grain, sound and titles together across the assembled work, each by one owner and against one look reference. Use the probe (Law 8) to establish that reference. Keep reference frames for each scene where useful and a color script for a long work when its look changes on purpose. Compare adjacent shots by sight and sound and measure properties that matter, such as levels, color balance, skin tones, loudness, voices and room tone. An underlying failure of world, scale or motion (Laws 4 to 6) must be repaired at its source, with the shot made again where necessary.

## Law 11. Judge the finished video, not the effort

The head judges the exact version intended for delivery, as the audience will see and hear it, against the brief and the applicable laws. That acceptance belongs to the finished version and must be established again after a repair.
