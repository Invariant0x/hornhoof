Use this ability with the laws of video production (vid-laws) for an assigned video. The head follows the people responsible for the department and owns the budget, the key's limits and final acceptance.

When setting up generation, ask for a video generation API key from an aggregator API with several video models or the model maker's API. Consumer subscriptions to video sites give agents no API access. Ask the responsible person to put the key in the vault, in the password field of a named entry for the service. Set the key's spending limit with the provider and record the amount and currency for the order. If the service has no cap, track remaining credit and stop calls before the limit can be exceeded. A person pays for credit or signs for the company. Agents take the key from the vault and sign in themselves. Keep it out of prompts, task records and git. The agent runs on its usual subscription, separate from the video service key. Set up the people and routes even when the key or subscription will come later.

Choose the pipeline that fits the brief. Use the default video-production pipeline for one short video and pick one keeper. For a film, a series or a work with many scenes or places, use video-long-work and pick one lead keeper for its shared sheets and sets and a keeper for each scene. Have the head and keeper accept the probe and set the look reference before full production.

On a long work, make the brief, story, bible, breakdown and probe once for the work. The bible holds character sheets, a set with markers for every place, the look book and color script, and the sound palette. Run scenes in parallel as separate tasks on video-production, from the scene script through the keeper's gate and edit. Use only the shared sheets, sets and look reference. Send any proposed bible change to the lead keeper and head, then pass the agreed change to every scene it touches. Assemble the scene edits into one cut before finishing.

Use one-shot calls to prepare independent draft shot prompts or compare draft takes. Give each call the brief, the applicable laws, the accepted reference frames and markers it needs. The operator signs in from the vault, runs the renders within the order's spending limit and checks the calls' answers. The keeper decides which shots can go to the editor.

Make shots from text, or from a first frame in the brief's look that the keeper accepted against the set's markers. Never animate a storyboard drawing, a sketch or a plain render of the set (Laws 1, 2 and 5). A prompt names the look in a few words, then gives the action by seconds, the camera and the sound, and ends with what the look must not be; most of its words go to action, not to appearance. Repeat the same wording for the look and for each character in every shot prompt. Make the first moving drafts at low resolution and give the keeper each version for review.

Check the draft cut before spending on final shots. After generation, the keeper checks the final shots before the editor replaces the draft shots. A scene task hands off its edit for assembly.

Finish the standalone video or assembled long work against the probe's look reference under Law 10. The colorist finishes color, contrast and grain, the sound designer makes the final mix, and the editor finishes titles. For a short video the head may give both finishing duties to the editor. The quality reviewer checks the finished work against the brief and look reference, and the head accepts it under Law 11 before mastering and delivery. Send, post or hand off as the department's work. Only irreversible deletion needs the CEO's yes. A slideshow of still images is never delivered as video.

Keep the brief, prompts, reference frames, accepted probe, shot decisions and the cost of each video in the department's git. Include the tool used, per-shot charges including retries, total and currency. Keep the files or their versioned locations with the record.
