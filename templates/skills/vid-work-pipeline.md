The head follows the people responsible for the department and directs the work within the agreed brief and budget. Set the audience, purpose, medium, look, required capture method and destination. Choose the route that suits the work, whether making footage, editing it or recording a screen. An unqualified film brief calls for live action, and a cartoon calls for animation. Use camera capture when the brief requires real people, places or events to be filmed. Agree on a frame and movement sample if a look is unclear. Fit the frame to the destination, keeping key details clear of controls and audible or readable as the viewing conditions require.

Check what the work requires and what the existing account provides. Consider a browser studio, a documented API, an official connector or command-line tool, or local tools. Check models, prices, limits and terms in the provider's current documentation and the account on the day of the choice. Check commercial use, reference rights, how uploads are used and how to export. Record the plan, region, terms, limits and price. No route is required: companies have different subscriptions, keys, hardware and rules. Compare the routes the company actually has by cost per accepted second at the quality the brief needs, counting drafts and retries, and recommend the cheapest route that meets the brief, with one alternative. Included credits, such as a studio's monthly credits or developer credits that come with a paid plan, are often the cheapest, and local tools can cost nothing but time. Check which route each benefit covers. The responsible person chooses, and the choice is recorded with the order.

An agent may operate a browser studio when the account holder authorizes it and the provider's terms do not forbid it. It works there like a person: on the account holder's assigned seat, one job at a time, at a person's pace, without scripts that press buttons, and it stops at any warning from the provider and asks the account holder. Where the terms forbid it, have an authorized person operate the studio or use a documented API or official connector that permits the work. The operator uses an assigned account and retrieves credentials from a named vault entry. The operator signs in at the protected sign-in surface or uses the authorized API. Ask the responsible person to arrange a missing seat or scoped credential. Send sign-in challenges that need the account holder to that person. Keep secrets out of prompts, commands, logs, task records and git.

Set the spending limit and currency for each order. Reserve the expected charge before submitting, use a service cap where possible and stop before the order can exceed its limit. Keep the job ID and check the existing job before retrying an uncertain submission. A person pays for credit or signs for the company. The agent keeps its usual subscription. Set up the people and route while missing access is arranged.

A tracker pipeline maps the work. The stage owner assigns each required check and records its owner, input and evidence. If there is no eligible keeper, the stage owner assigns a qualified substitute. Keep the result in progress until the exact version has the required acceptance, even if the tracker can advance without it. Assign acceptance of the probe, the clips, the draft cut and the finished work.

The writer states the premise and story line in a sentence: whose goal or question carries the work, what stands in the way and what changes. For work without a plot, use the viewer's task, question or intended progression. Put beats in viewing order. State each scene objective, what changes and what the audience knows on entry and exit. Link setups and payoffs so a revision reaches both. The director approves the story line, shot plan, designed world, preferred performances and final creative cut.

Name a cinematographer for framing, camera and light and a production designer for sets, materials, props and the look of characters. A qualified operator or board artist can take the corresponding duty. The director, cinematographer, operator and editor agree on the shot list: number each shot, name its beat and planned cut, and specify the shot size, angle, lens or field of view, focus, camera movement, action on entry and exit, sound cue and usable duration. A wide shot orients, a close shot carries a detail or reaction. Camera position sets perspective. Give a move a reason and a start and end. Plan coverage the edit needs, such as a full view, a reaction or an insert, with overlap for cuts. Play timed boards or rough performances with scratch sound where timing matters.

Use motivated light: agree on what appears to light the scene and match its direction, softness, color and contrast across angles. Design and photography build the look book with the colorist, who maps intended changes through the story in the color script. Label approved references by what they control, such as identity, set, motion or style. Keep their source, rights and version. Prepare character views, set plans and starting or ending frames as needed, and check new angles before making moving shots.

Plan sound from the start with the sound designer. Mark which beats need clean dialogue or narration, room tone, effects, music or silence. Gather the separate tracks the edit needs and mark temporary tracks. Check intelligibility, sync and how sound carries across a cut.

Adjust the plan to the chosen look:

- **Live action.** Plan performance, blocking, lenses, light and coverage. For a generated photographic look, check skin and fabric in motion, shadows, focus and contact with objects.
- **3D animation.** Share models, rigs, materials, set scale and virtual cameras. Approve rough layout and movement before detailed animation, lighting and rendering.
- **2D animation.** Set silhouette, proportions, expressions, line and palette. Time the key poses and test movement before clean drawings and inbetweens.
- **Anime.** Set the specific character and background style, effects and how they combine. Check key drawings, held poses and changes in speed before inbetweens.
- **Stop motion.** Fix puppet or object scale, camera and exposure. Check each pose against its neighbor for set bumps and flicker. Keep the stepped motion and the captured frames when physical capture is required.
- **Motion graphics.** Approve frames with the real words and figures. Time entrances, changes and reading holds to the sound where used. Keep exact type, marks and geometry editable.
- **Screen recording.** Rehearse the real task at the chosen window size and zoom. Keep the pointer path, wording and visible result clear at delivery size.

Before using a model, the operator reads the maker's current prompt guide and writes in the model's structure. Follow its advice for subject, action, camera, lens, light, style and audio as applicable. Supply approved references or starting and ending frames where supported. For a continuation, carry forward an accepted frame or clip, specify the next action and play across the join. Use cheaper modes for drafts that still show the risks, and the quality mode for accepted finals that need a full render.

Prove the hardest shot or edit before full production. When continuity or timing is at risk, play the join with both shots and their sound. When the choice is uncertain, compare methods within budget using the same references and acceptance conditions. The director and keeper accept the exact probe and set the shared baseline. Record the chosen route, model, settings, look reference, cost and fallback. Test a materially different risk where it appears.

For long work the lead keeper keeps the approved story, character sheets, sets, look book, color script and sound palette in one production bible. Give each parallel scene its own keeper and the shared references. Send story, performance and visual changes through the director and lead keeper, and pass on the agreed revision to every affected scene, including any linked setup or payoff.

Use one-shot calls for written options such as shot prompts. Give the calls the requirements and the evidence they can read. A visual verdict comes from a checker who opened and played the exact media. Review takes together while the setup or references are available. The keeper marks usable ranges, the director picks performances, and the editor tries uncertain joins and names the smallest pickup that can fix a gap. Recheck replacement clips before the editor uses them.

Play the assembly for missing information, the rough cut for story and performance, and the fine cut for timing and joins. Leave review notes after each pass with the version, time range, intended effect and repair owner. Cut on action where movement should carry the eye. A J cut brings in the next sound early, and an L cut lets the current sound continue over the next shot. Use music, exact artwork, titles and end cards where the brief needs them. Keep usable drafts and recordings and commission only the finals or pickups the checked edit needs. The director locks picture for finishing.

The colorist matches shots within each intended look, the sound designer mixes to the verified destination requirements, and the editor finishes picture, titles and captions as needed. For a short video the head may give both finishing duties to the editor when that person can do the work. Compare any upscale or new frame rate with the accepted source in motion. Each specialist confirms their contribution to the version the editor exports. The quality reviewer and director play the exact exported master at normal speed in the intended conditions and check picture, sound, sync and any captions. Step through frames to diagnose specific issues. Recheck a corrected export and deliver the accepted version.

Keep the brief, prompts, reference frames, accepted probe, clip decisions and cost in the department's git. For each attempt record the route, model, settings, reference versions, job ID where available, exact take and actual cost. Reconcile the total and currency, counting retries, finishing and the share of plan costs. Record operator time. Keep a short note per model in the department's notebook with what worked, settings, cost per accepted second and the probe file. Record when a probe produced no usable footage. Choose tools from this evidence.
