# A conveyor for any department: fast and high quality

Date: 2026-11-04. Author: David AI, from Ivan's words in the session of 2026-11-04. Version 3: after a critical review by
Antigravity (Google) checked against the templates and the engine, and with Ivan's rulings on tests (section 4a).
Status: for turn 8, which must solve this whole task: speed, quality and correctness.

## 1. Intent

Ivan: "build a conveyor that turns out versions from different models like mad, merges them just as fast without long
delays, and is insanely high quality at the same time." It must work for every department, not only Development: sales
with its endless emails, support, marketing, business development, finance, design, operations, home devices and the
household. It must live in the templates, roles and skills every new company gets, take each model's habits into
account, and run like clockwork, without the failures and stuck terminals we see today.

Goals:
- Shorter time from a task given to a result accepted, in every department, measured against today (section 11).
- Quality no lower than today: no check is dropped, only moved to where it costs least.
- The same rules for every company that installs Hornhoof, and for any project, language or channel.
- Every stage measured, so the next bottleneck is found from numbers, not guesses.

Non-goals: new departments; replacing the task engine (it changes where section 10 says, it is not rewritten); changing
what the CEO decides about money and goals.

Anti-goals (a failure even if it "works"): a check skipped silently or counted as passed without running; the CEO or a
head becoming the queue every item waits in; an agent waiting with nobody and no clock owning the wait; per-item human
approval coming back as the default; a writer model checking its own claims and calling that a check.

- Waiting for test runs: 11 waits, about 6 h in total, 20 to 90 min each (a separate remote run per task, on loaded

- Waiting for mutator runs by writers: about 3.5 h; one task (#242) waited almost 3 h.
- 14 agent turns cut by machine restarts (updates at 14:26 and 16:10).
- Hand-ins per task: 1.0. Returns from checks, 40% in turn 2, are no longer the problem.

Writers and reviewers, measured from their own logs:
- Google's review of a 22-file change: 25 and 35 min, 75% and 91% of it model thinking over 150 to 250 steps. Its second
  review re-read the whole change, not only the fixes.
- Google's last fix round (writing code): 120 min, of which mutator 54 min (45%), thinking 49 min, tests 12 min.

Tests, measured by the network build on home-api and by me:
- The whole Hornhoof suite: about 3900 tests in 251 files, about 65 min of worker time, 26 min real time on 3 CPUs.
  A typical server test takes 1.5 to 2 s: each opens its own database from a dump and builds the whole app.
- Mutator reruns the whole test command for every mutant, so its cost is the tests' cost. Against those tests it took

Conclusion: the time goes to heavy checks repeated per writer and per round, to runs queued on loaded machines, to cut
turns, and to reviews that re-read everything. Not to the work itself.

## 3. Principles, the same for every department

P1. Three levels of checks.
- Author level, per item, minutes, before handing in. Wherever a check can be code, it is code, not the writer's own
  judgement (development: types, lint, tests of what changed, crapper, dryer; sales: the outbound gate of section 6).
  A hand-in that fails them never reaches a reviewer.
- Batch level, once per turn or per batch: the expensive checks run once on everything together (development: the full
  suite in parts, the turn review, QA acceptance; sales: sampled review of a batch). Mutator is not a batch check of
  everything: it runs only on the riskiest pure logic with its fast tests (section 4a).
  Findings go straight to the author of the line or item.
- Improvement lanes, never blocking: specialists who look at what was released and find what to make better
  (performance, adversarial hunting, deliverability, knowledge base). They file normal tasks for a later turn, with a
  measurement that becomes the test.

P2. Every wait has an owner and a clock. An item waits only on a named agent or person with a deadline; at the deadline
the wait escalates by rule (section 7). This covers every kind of wait, not only `wait_for`: waiting after another task,
for a review, for a request or answer, for a run. Mutual waits are detected (#235).

P3. Approval is a policy of the department's owner, not of the CEO (Ivan, 2026-11-04). The owner sets, per channel or
kind of work: "each item to me", "new kinds only", or "no approval within these limits". Not every department has its
own person: today the CEO owns them all, and the CEO can name a person as the owner of any department at any time. A
department without a named owner is the CEO's, so the CEO sets its policy and gets what that policy sends; this includes
release approval in Development today. Once a department has its own owner, the CEO is involved only when it misses its
goals or plan (no customers, no deals, releases late), never per item. Naming or changing an owner keeps the current
policy until the new owner changes it. The owner is a field of the department, set by the CEO, not guessed from who has
access.

P4. Cut work so parallel authors do not collide: one owner per file area, account, segment or ticket queue in a turn.
Collisions are found when work is given out, not at merge time. Hotspot files every feature touches (string tables,
schema, route lists) are changed by one task at a time.

P5. Model-aware by stage. Effort follows the stage, not only the role: today's levels stay for plans, first reviews and
security (xhigh where the role has it now); re-checks and mechanical fixes run lower. A program takes its effort when it
starts, so the level is picked per wake, never switched inside a running turn. Reviews use one reviewer per provider,
never the provider that wrote the part. Each program's known failures are handled by the one failure mechanism; a program
takes a role only after its readiness checks pass (section 8).

P6. Generic templates, specific descriptions. Templates hold the rules; each product, channel or queue has a short
description written by its owner role in the first turn (the test environment of #280 is the model; the same for a sales
channel or a support queue). New companies get the rules at once and the descriptions in their first turn.

P7. Measure every stage and report it at turn close (section 9).

P8. State lives in Hornhoof, not in a chat. Area locks, deadlines, owner policies and what waits for whom are task and
department state in the database, given back to an agent after it compacts. A head's notebook is for its thinking, not
for rules the engine enforces.

## 4. Development

Already decided or planned (keep): the dev pool, one turn review instead of per-task gates, the full suite once per turn,
the shared test environment described by QA (#280), fast tests (#222), mutator's false 100% (#237), CI without a second
run (#221),
mutual waits (#235), updates that wait for agents to finish (#298), the program operator (#234). The turn review stays
one round (Ivan): its fixes start with a failing test and are covered by the final full suite and QA acceptance, not by
a second review.

New:
- D1. Author tests by impact. The author runs only the tests that depend on the changed files, found by Hornhoof from
  the import graph or the test runner's own "related" mode, on the author's machine in the environment of #280. When a
  change touches what an import graph does not see (migrations, schema, configuration, environment variables, route
  lists, fixtures, string tables), the author runs the whole package's tests, and migrations also up and down. The
  final full suite of the turn still catches the rest. Depends on #280 and #222.
- D2. A fast suite. Measure first where a test's time goes (opening the database, building the app, the test itself).
  If opening a database per test dominates, as it seems, one database per test file with a cheap reset between tests,
  and one built app per file, done once in the shared test helpers, as its own task; tests that depend on ids,
  sequences or time are found and fixed in the same task. Then the runner's settings (pool, isolation, workers). The
  test timeout is passed so that it reaches the runner (today `npm test -- --testTimeout` from the root does not).
- D3. Reviews that do not re-read everything. Hornhoof stores the commit each review looked at; a second look diffs
  from that commit to the new one and checks only the fixes against the first findings. A change larger than about 800
  lines is split by area between two reviewers at once, each of a provider that did not write that area. The reviewer
  gets the author's evidence (tests run, gate numbers) and does not rerun them.
- D4. Effort by stage (P5) in the dev roles and skills: plans, first reviews and security keep today's level;
  re-checks and mechanical fixes run at medium.
- D5. Merge in batches. Tasks merged into the turn branch in a batch get one run of the affected tests on a free company
  machine. A red batch is bisected by Hornhoof; a failure that comes from two tasks together (neither breaks alone) goes
  to the head of Development, not to the second author.
- D6. Improvement lanes out of the conveyor. DEV-PERF is already not a gate and is off by default; it becomes a lane that
  measures each released version (startup, key requests, memory, test time) and files tasks with before and after
  numbers, on any models. The turn's final check adds a cheap benchmark of a few key scenarios, so a slowdown does not
  slip through while the lane looks elsewhere. DEV-SEC and DEV-RED are woken for the turn review only when the diff
  touches their paths (access, secrets, sign-in, permissions, network boundaries, given as path patterns in their role),
  and then before the release; on other turns DEV-RED works as a lane on the released version.
- D7. An urgent lane (the tiger team): a small team ships a small urgent fix with all checks, including mutator on its
  pure logic with its fast tests (T4), and its own release (0.6.1 was made this way). It branches from main next to the open turn, releases,
  and its fix is merged back into the open turn branch at once. The engine allows this lane beside the one open turn.
- D8. Writers never wait for heavy machines while idle: while a batch check runs, the author takes the next task in a
  separate worktree (dev-worktrees joins the DEV-ENG skills).
- D9. A plan limit in the middle of a task does not freeze it. On a usage limit the node commits and pushes the work in
  progress to the task's branch, so a stand-in of the same role on another provider can go on from it. Today a stand-in
  takes only work nobody started.

## 4a. Tests, the right way, for any project and language (Ivan, 2026-11-04)

These rules go into the templates for every company and any language, not only Hornhoof.

- T1. Tests by layer, the Uncle Bob way without chasing coverage. Logic lives in pure functions with direct, fast tests
  (milliseconds each, no database, no network, no processes). Each route or seam with the outside world gets as few
  integration tests as cover its contract and its named failure modes, usually one or two. A few end-to-end checks
  cover what a person does. No test is written only to cover a line or raise a number. To bring a function under CRAP
  10, split it first. PGlite has one connection and runs as a superuser, so what depends on real Postgres (locks and
  races, role permissions, a migration over existing data) gets a few tests on real Postgres in the final run.
- T2. A task runs only what its change reaches: the tests that import the changed files (`vitest related`, `jest
  --findRelatedTests`, `pytest --testmon`, `go test` of the changed packages, `cargo test -p` and so on), types, lint,
  crapper and dryer on the changed functions. A change to migrations, schema, configuration, environment variables,
  routes, fixtures, string tables, scripts or the install and update path runs its whole package at once. Selection is
  measured, not trusted: if a shared test helper imports the whole app, "related" picks almost every test, so heavy
  helpers are split from light ones; a new test file always runs; when unsure, the package runs. A failure the final
  run finds that selection missed is logged as a selection miss and counted (section 9).
- T3. One table of checks, the same in every template:
  - during work: the change's related tests, types, lint, crapper and dryer (T2);
  - after a batch merge: the affected tests of the batch on one pinned commit;
  - final candidate of the turn: the whole suite (root `npm test` with test typechecks and each touched app's typecheck),
    the build, the browser checks QA needs and the real-Postgres tests (T1), split across free machines.
  Never per task or per fix round. Every check names the one commit it ran on: Hornhoof resolves the commit before it
  hands out the parts of a run and every part tests exactly that commit (today each part clones the branch and may get a
  newer one). A result counts as evidence only while its commit is the one under review.
- T4. Mutator (and Stryker, PIT, Infection, mull for other languages) runs once per release or phase, only on the
  riskiest pure logic, and only against that logic's own fast tests. Never per task, never per fix round, never against
  tests with a database, real processes or a network: it reruns the tests for every mutant, so its cost is the tests'
  cost. Where the tool can run only the tests that cover each mutant and remember earlier results (Stryker's per-test
  coverage and incremental mode), that is used. Survivors go to the author as direct tests; an equivalent mutant is
  ruled on with why.
  Which logic is "riskiest" is a rule, not a reviewer's taste: the pure functions the release changed (no import of a
  database, network or processes), ordered by CRAP and size of change, plus everything on the paths of money, access
  and security, within a time budget set as a share of the release check. The report lists killed, survived, not
  covered, invalid (does not compile) and timed out separately; a compile failure is not a kill (#237). Stryker's
  Vitest runner gets a pilot against our pinned versions; its incremental cache is dropped when a helper, the
  configuration, a dependency or the environment changes.
- T5. Old slow tests are not rewritten in bulk. New code follows T1; a slow test is rewritten only when its code
  changes anyway.
- T6. Reviews ask for extra tests only with a named harm to a person; "coverage is low" alone is no finding. A bug fix
  still starts with a failing test that reproduces the bug; T6 is about extra tests a review asks for.
- T7. The suite's own speed is measured every turn (section 9) and owned by the performance lane (D6): time per package
  and per part, and the tests slower than their package's median by a set factor (relative, never in minutes). Elapsed
  time on the critical path is reported apart from summed hours of all agents.
- T8. Every department's checks are tested the same way (section 6): a check written as code has fast unit tests,
  including the cases it must refuse; a check done by a model (a reply classifier, a brand and claims review, a design
  critique) has a versioned set of examples with known answers, labeled by someone other than its author, plus a held
  back part the check's author never sees. It runs when the check, its prompt or the provider's model changes, not on
  every item, and reports false accepts and false rejects separately. The department owner sets the threshold at which
  a change of the check is accepted. Every error found later in real work becomes a new example. A clean small set
  proves little: zero failures in 50 examples is still consistent with about 6% failures.
- T9. Tests are deterministic (F.I.R.S.T.: fast, independent, repeatable, self-verifying, timely). A test does not
  depend on the date, the time zone, the order of tests, randomness or ids; the shared helpers set the clock, the time
  zone and the seed. A flaky test is a bug fixed at its cause: no retries, no quarantine. Once per turn the final run
  shuffles the order to prove independence. (restarts.test.ts broke on the date and Department.test.tsx on the time
  zone in the last day.)
- T10. Evidence is a Hornhoof run, not a retelling. A task is handed in with the id of its run_tests run on a named
  commit; the reviewer checks that id and does not rerun the tests. A parser that marks every result a success (#312)
  is a bug in the evidence itself.
- T11. Crapper and dryer reuse the coverage of the run already made and never start another coverage run. A
  changed function over CRAP 10 is split (the ub-mode gate stays); a dryer flag is evidence for the reviewer, removed
  or kept with a written reason, and dryer compares the changed code with the code already there too.

## 4b. How code gets written: our cycle (v4)

Ivan's idea: first make the logic work, then decompose it, cut what is extra and the small leftovers, then cover it
with tests. The reviewers' verdict, checked against Beck, North and Feathers: it is the right order when the way is
unknown (a new integration, an unfamiliar API, a screen), and it is Dan North's "spike and stabilize". Its weak spot is
that tests written after the code by the same author copy the code's mistakes. Strict test-first is faster and safer
when the behaviour is known and for bugs. So the head marks each task with one of four kinds, and the kind picks the
cycle:

1. Bug: a failing test that reproduces it, then the fix (always, T6).
2. Known behaviour: test-first in small steps, red, green, clean (dev-tdd).
3. Unknown way: one acceptance test written from the requirement before any code (what a person sees or what the API
   returns), then Ivan's order inside it: make it work as a timeboxed spike, decompose the logic into pure functions,
   cut the extra, then direct fast tests on the pure functions (T1). The acceptance test, written first, is what keeps
   decomposition honest and stops the later tests from just copying the code. A spike that is thrown away needs no
   tests.
4. Old code to change: characterization tests first (Feathers: they pin what the code does today, surprises
   included), then structural commits apart from behaviour commits (Beck's Tidy First), then the behaviour change
   against its own test.

Around it, the same for every kind:
- The head writes for each task: what a person observes when it is done (the acceptance), the risk class (money,
  access, security, data, or none) and the kind. The risk class, not only paths, wakes DEV-SEC and DEV-RED (D6); path
  patterns stay as an extra trigger.
- The author hands in with T10 evidence. One review per turn stays (Ivan). For a blocking finding on security, data
  or the spec only, the reviewer who raised it reads the fix's delta (D3, `reviewed_commit..fix_commit`) at medium
  effort, minutes, not a new review; other fixes are covered by their failing-first tests, the final run and QA.
- Each provider's reviewer counts its confirmed findings per turn; the performance lane reports a reviewer that adds
  nothing, and the owner decides. Provider diversity is kept (Ivan) and measured, not assumed.
- Work in progress is bounded: an author holds at most one task in work and one in checks (D8); a red turn branch goes
  before any new task.
- Requirement-to-test evidence (from ECC's verification loop) and verification before any claim (superpowers) go into
  dev-implement and dev-review-verdict; universal coverage thresholds such as 80% are not taken.

Measuring which cycle is fastest. Codex proposed 216 runs, Google 36; we take a smaller paired experiment plus live
numbers:
- Live: every development task in turns 8 and 9 carries its kind, and the performance lane reports per kind the elapsed
  time to acceptance, review returns, fix rounds and bugs found later in that code.
- Experiment, at the end of turn 8 after D2 lands, on one commit: three ways, (W1) strict test-first, (W2) Ivan's order
  with no test first, (W3) the hybrid above (acceptance test first, then Ivan's order). Six real tasks: two bugs, two
  pure-logic modules, two features through database, route and screen. 6 x 3 = 18 runs in separate worktrees, split
  between a Claude and a Codex writer and counterbalanced, at the same effort. Before the runs DEV-TEST writes a hidden
  acceptance and adversarial suite from each brief; reviewers of a third provider review blind to the way.
- Numbers: elapsed minutes to a green author check, tokens, test runs, fix rounds, lead time through review; quality as
  the hidden suite's pass rate, blind blocking findings, mutation score on the pure logic, CRAP.
- Rule: a way with a lower hidden-suite pass rate or more escaped blocking bugs than W1 is dropped; among the rest, the
  one with at least 15% shorter median lead time wins, overall or per kind. The owner of Development sets the default
  cycle per kind from this and the live numbers after turn 9.

## 6. The other departments

How each department's checks are tested: T8. The outbound gate, the reply gate and every other check in code have fast
unit tests with the cases that must never pass (a price not on the list, a flagged recipient, a missing opt-out). The
model checks (ticket classifier, brand and claims review, design critique, finance recompute) each keep a set of known
examples, including the hard ones, and are rerun on it when the check, its prompt or its model changes.

Where a per-item check stays: a mistake that cannot be undone (money, legal, access, a physical action), a risk that
adds up item by item (a sending domain's reputation, spam complaints), or a check that no code can do. Everything else
moves to the batch level.

Sales (and business development outreach, which uses the same gate):
- An outbound gate in code checks every message before it can leave: prices, discounts and terms only from the approved
  price list; links and claims only from the approved lists; the recipient has no do-not-contact or missing-consent flag
  in the CRM; an opt-out is present; daily caps per mailbox and domain. The writer cannot pass it by saying so.
- Then one fresh-context check by another provider (brand and fit, claims and rules), per message while the channel is
  new. Cold outreach has no name-swapped templates (sal-cold-outreach), so what the owner approves is an approach: the
  segment, the claims used, the structure and the ask. The owner's policy decides when per-message checks become
  samples: for example after 50 clean messages in a segment, then the first 5 of each new segment or SDR and 1 in 10
  after. A failed sample or a rise in bounces or complaints stops the channel and returns it to per-message checks.
- Sending runs continuously on timers within the caps; no message waits for a person unless the policy says so.
- Replies about price, contract or legal terms go to the department's owner; the CEO only by goals.
- Accounts are locked to one SDR or BD agent at a time, so two agents never write to the same company.
- Improvement lane: deliverability (bounces, spam placement, domain health), list hygiene, A/B tests of approaches.
- Today's sal-outbound-approval holds every message for two reviews and CEO approval until the CEO lifts it per
  channel; mkt-publish-approval holds every post for the CEO with no lift at all. Both change to the owner's policy.

Marketing publishing: the same outbound gate for claims and links, the owner's policy per channel.

Support: a separate classifier (not the writer) sorts each ticket; refunds, legal, data requests and anything the policy
names go to a person whatever the reply looks like. Replies to known categories go out after the reply gate by the
owner's policy. An SLA clock per ticket escalates by rule; the knowledge base lane turns repeated tickets into articles
(sup-docs-from-tickets).

Finance keeps maker and checker per item where money leaves the company or the books close, and on every forecast or
model shown to the CEO or used for a spending decision (fin-independent-recompute).

Design: the author checks tokens, contrast and the spec's completeness before handing to Development; DSN-QA stays the
batch check of the turn.

Operations: a change to a live machine, plan or connector carries a rollback step and a check, and one that can stop
other agents' work is approved per item by the owner (blast radius).

Home devices and household: every physical action or purchase stays per item (home-real-world-approval); planning and
research move to the batch level.

## 7. Escalation

- Every wait carries a deadline and a level. A periodic sweep moves an item over its deadline to the stage owner, then
  the head, then the department's owner (the CEO when the department has no named owner).
- The CEO gets each department's goal and plan status at turn close, and an alert only when a goal is missed or at risk
  (no new customers this week, releases late, SLA breached repeatedly).
- Operations (OPS-WATCH, OPS-BUGS, the program operator) handles what the failure mechanism could not heal; it does not
  handle business decisions.

## 8. Models and programs

- One reviewer per provider; never the provider that wrote the part.
- Each role's template states which programs and models suit it and the effort per stage (P5).
- A program takes a role only after its readiness checks pass: its event parser verified on a live run, real tool exit
  codes, its own configuration per session, a way to review without subagents where the skill expects them, and a way to
  restart it when it hangs. Today Kimi's parser is unverified and marks every tool result as passed, and Antigravity has
  no restart rule.
- Known failures and their handling, all through the one failure mechanism: a program asking a person (the operator,
  #234); plan limits (wait for the reset, or D9 and a stand-in of the same role on another provider; a limit with no
  known reset goes to a stand-in); crashes (one continuation); lost live words (0.6.2); restarts during updates (#298);
  long silent tools (silence alarms with the tool's own limit); Codex refusing security work by its policy (that work
  goes to another provider); a program ending its turn after only promising to act (the turn-end reminder).
- Model habits go into the skills: Codex runs long commands in the background and must collect their results before it
  reports; Antigravity thinks long per step, so its briefs are narrow and name the files, and its instructions stay
  under its 24 KB limit (skills are read on demand, not pasted in); Claude keeps context well and takes the long-session
  roles (heads, orchestrator), with the rules it must keep held as state (P8), not only in its context.
- Before a stand-in takes a plan's work, Hornhoof checks which machine holds that plan's lease.

## 9. Measurement

- Hornhoof derives per-agent time (work, waiting, person, stall, lost) from task events and jobs (tree-facts,
  SpeedPanel), but today only for the open turn and without test runs. It changes to take any turn, to count waits for
  test runs and their parts and each kind of outside wait separately, and to save a snapshot of the turn when it closes,
  before the next one opens.
- At turn close Hornhoof writes a short conveyor report per department: lead time median and worst, time per stage,
  waits by cause, returns, failures, cut turns, each compared with the turn before in percent. The orchestrator reads it
  and the CEO sees it.
- The conveyor's own speed belongs to the performance lane (D6), not only the product's: its engineers read the report,
  take the biggest wait as a task for the next turn and show the cut in percent.
- Department metrics from section 5 (reply rate, time to first reply and so on) come from the department's own tools
  through its description (P6).

Engine (the conveyor needs these; the templates alone cannot do them):
